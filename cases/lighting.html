Igors worked on

Making light work

Designing a management system for outdoor lighting that helps save energy and reduce maintenance costs.

Year

2021

Duration

300h

Role

Research, ideation, validation, prototyping.

Problem

Running a city's lighting network means translating municipal goals, such as saving energy, cutting maintenance costs and keeping streets safe, into daily schedules and repairs across thousands of luminaires.

Two primary users hold different mental models of that work, and most of the design sits in the hand-off between them. The control-room operator works top-down, from policy to zone, schedule, group and luminaire. The field technician works bottom-up, from ticket to module, pole and proof of repair. A fault has to travel from one world to the other and back: detected, triaged, dispatched, fixed, verified and closed.

At city scale, every luminaire becomes a source of telemetry and potential alerts. The day-to-day challenge for the operator is surfacing faults that need action without drowning in noise, seeing energy and usage data clearly enough to adjust schedules, and keeping repair workflows short.

Who we designed for

Primary users

People who work with the system directly.

Secondary users

People who set the goals of the system or depend on what it produces.

Urban designers and architects

Integrate smart lighting with broader urban design projects.

Environmental analysts

Track environmental impact and progress against sustainability targets.

Community members

Rely on street lighting for safety, convenience, and quality of life.

Policy makers and government officials

Set municipal lighting and energy policies and account for the results.

Constraints

Decade-long lifecycles

Lighting hardware stays in the ground for 10+ years. The design had to assume maintainability, durability and future upgradability rather than a typical software refresh cycle.

Towns to metropolitan areas

The same system had to hold up from a few hundred luminaires to citywide networks, without small deployments inheriting big-city complexity.

Field
conditions

Technicians work outdoors on mobile devices. Anything they need has to work in that context.

Approach

Modelling the domain

Before any screens, the work was to settle what the system is made of. The industry has a standards-backed vocabulary you can use.1 From those standards and the day-to-day work of running the network, the domain model settled into three layers: what is in the ground, how it is programmed, and what it reports back.

The TALQ Smart City Protocol models the world as Device → Function → Attribute/Event, addressed by Groups and driven by Services. D4i defines the luminaire, energy and diagnostics data each device reports, and EN 13201 supplies the lighting-class taxonomy.

Asset layer

Poles, brackets, luminaires, drivers and controllers: the physical stuff, each with its own attributes and settings.

Control layer

Groups, zones and segments act as the targets of commands, with schedules and dimming profiles as reusable programs assigned to them.

Runtime layer

Live telemetry, faults reported from the network, and the maintenance tasks opened to resolve them.2

Faults include dimming failures, lost communication, voltage anomalies and day burners: lamps left lit in daylight.

Navigating lighting infrastructure

The asset layer is what an operator navigates. It appears two ways at once, as a hierarchical list and on an interactive map, both carrying live status, because the same question arrives sometimes by identifier and sometimes by location.

Lighting maps invert the usual cartographic priorities. A general-purpose base map gives its contrast to roads, landmarks and retail; here those are stripped back until the lighting infrastructure is the primary visual element across the three zoom bands the network is administered in.

Energy profiles

Schedules and dimming patterns are the control layer's programs. A profile is defined on its own and does nothing until an area references it, so updating how a district runs at night happens in one place rather than per luminaire.

Metric visualisation

How runtime data is drawn depends on what the operator is doing with it. Independent readings that share only a timestamp get stacked panels, each with its current value and its own scale: an air quality sensor's temperature, humidity, pressure and gas concentrations have no common unit and no reason to share an axis.

When a device reports across several subsystems (controller health, luminaire, motion sensor, energy meter, LED), a comparison mode lets the operator check any combination of series to overlay them on a single timeline with a shared crosshair. Plotting brightness against counted objects is how an operator checks whether a street's light level is justified by its traffic.

Reports

Reports aggregate the runtime layer over the control layer's areas: worked hours, nominal energy, real energy and the resulting economy, per area and per month.

Key decisions

A profile is one list of rules

Rather than splitting a schedule into a nightly curve and a separate calendar of which curve runs on which day, a profile keeps days and brightness rules in a single ordered list. That matches how operators describe a street ("full light at dusk, 75% late in the evening, except on Friday nights"), and lets the chart above the list preview any night of the week directly.

One app for the control room and the field

The control room and the field share one responsive web app instead of separate desktop and mobile tools. A technician registers a new lamp, scans its QR code and logs a repair in the same application the operator uses to triage faults, so both sides look at the same devices, tasks and history with nothing copied between systems.

Profiles belong to places, not lamps

Lamps inherit their profile from their area, while sensor-dependent modifiers (traffic boost, daylight adjustment) apply only where a lamp has the hardware and are ignored where it doesn't. A street with a mix of older and newer poles still runs from a single profile.3

The trade-off is that cross-area policies have to be assigned per lamp, and the profile preview shows the ideal curve rather than what an individual sensorless lamp will do.

Impact

A short, fixed-scope engagement doesn't leave room to validate a system like this in deployment, so the measure is what the design targets: the operator behaviours it makes possible.

Alarm load

Signals are prioritised and grouped, with informational events separated from faults that require action, so the steady-state alarm rate stays manageable.

Fault to repair

A fault travels cleanly from detection to closure (triaged into a work order, dispatched, fixed and verified) with nothing rekeyed at the operator-to-technician hand-off.

Right light, not more light

Operators can set and defend lighting to policy by level, place and time, rather than defaulting to maximum brightness on the assumption that brighter is safer.

Learnings

IoT ecosystem knowledge

Working through the full stack of a connected-infrastructure product gave me practical grounding in how sensors, connectivity limits, power budgets and telemetry shape what an interface can reliably show and promise.

Standards as design material

Industry standards turned out to be a source of information architecture, not just compliance: a vocabulary the users' tools and vendors already speak, free to adopt and cheaper to maintain than a custom ontology.
